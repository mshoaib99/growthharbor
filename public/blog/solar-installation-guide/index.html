Solar Installation: A Practical Guide for Homeowners

Updated October 6, 2026 · General information

Solar installation can look complicated when you first start researching it. There are panels, inverters, roof measurements, electrical work, permits and financing options to think about. The good news is that the process becomes much easier to understand when you break it into a few clear stages.

This guide walks through the basics so you can approach a solar project with better questions and a clearer idea of what a professional installer should explain.

Before you start: Solar requirements, incentives, utility rules and installation practices vary by location. Use this article as general background and confirm project-specific details with qualified local professionals and your utility.

1. Start with the home, not the panels

A good solar project begins with the property. An installer will normally look at the roof's size, orientation, pitch, age and condition, along with shading from trees or nearby structures. Electrical service and available installation space can matter too.

If the roof needs major work soon, it may be worth resolving that first. Removing and reinstalling a solar array later can add unnecessary cost and disruption.

2. Understand the basic equipment

A typical residential system includes solar modules, an inverter or microinverters, mounting hardware and electrical equipment. Some homes also add a battery for energy storage.

The right configuration depends on the home's energy use, roof characteristics, utility requirements and the homeowner's goals. A larger system is not automatically a better system if it is poorly matched to the property.

3. Ask how the system is sized

Ask the installer to explain how the proposed system size was calculated. A useful proposal should connect the recommendation to historical electricity use, available roof area, expected production and local conditions.

Do not be afraid to ask for the assumptions behind the numbers. You should be able to understand what the estimate is based on without needing to be an engineer.

4. Compare the proposal, not just the price

Two quotes can have very different prices while appearing similar at first glance. Compare the equipment included, warranty terms, installation scope, monitoring, expected production, financing costs and any work that is excluded.

Look carefully for items that could become additional charges later. A lower headline price is not necessarily the lower overall cost.

5. Check permits, utility requirements and timelines

Residential solar often involves permits, inspections and coordination with the local utility. The exact process varies by jurisdiction. Ask who is responsible for each step and what could delay the project.

It is also useful to ask when installation is expected to begin, what happens if weather causes a delay and when the system is expected to be ready for operation.

6. Think about maintenance and monitoring

Modern solar systems generally need limited routine maintenance, but homeowners should still understand how performance is monitored and what happens if an inverter or panel develops a problem.

Ask how service requests are handled, how warranty claims work and whether monitoring is included.

7. Questions worth asking an installer

  • How did you determine the recommended system size?
  • What equipment is included in the proposal?
  • What is covered by the equipment and workmanship warranties?
  • Who handles permits and utility paperwork?
  • What work is excluded from the quoted price?
  • How will system performance be monitored?
  • What happens if the roof needs repair later?

Final thoughts

The best solar decision is usually an informed one rather than a rushed one. Gather more than one proposal when practical, ask questions about anything you do not understand and verify important claims before signing an agreement.
